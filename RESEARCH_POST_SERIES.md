### POST 2 — "The paper our name is built on"

- **Post text:**
  > Our "Genetic Goldmine" name didn't come from nowhere. It traces back to a real study: Seeley, Tarpy, Griffin, Carcione, and Delaney (2015) went into New York's Arnot Forest — where wild honey bee colonies have been surviving on their own for decades — and genotyped a population of them to investigate their genetic structure. Dr. Tarpy is our own scientific advisor; Dr. Delaney's research goes back to the very first version of this project in 2008.
  >
  > That's the whole idea behind SaveTheHives: every wild colony we map is a potential subject for exactly this kind of research. A pin on our map today could be a data point in someone's study tomorrow. savethehives.org

### POST 5 — "The bees already doing what breeders are trying to engineer"

- **Post text:**
  > Right now, breeding programs around the country are working to engineer Varroa Sensitive Hygiene (VSH) into managed honey bees — teaching them to detect and remove mite-infested brood before it becomes a problem. It's careful, deliberate, expensive work.
  >
  > Here's the thing: some wild colonies may already have figured this out on their own, through years of natural selection with zero human help. We don't know which ones until someone finds them, logs them, and checks back. That's the entire reason this map exists. savethehives.org
- **Image:** `images/honeybee-on-comb.jpg`
- **Alt text:** "Close-up of a honey bee on honeycomb."
- **Link:** trailing plain text
- **Source:** `FACEBOOK_STARTER_PACK.md` §6 ("Varroa Sensitive Hygiene (VSH) breeding programs — managed breeding is trying to engineer the exact trait wild survivor colonies may already have naturally. Good 'two paths to the same goal' framing.") — general, well-established breeding-program concept, not tied to a single named study, so no specific citation needed beyond that framing note.
